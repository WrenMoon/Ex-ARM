\title{Active Proprioceptive Recognition of Object Shape and Size with a
LEAP Hand: A Simulation-Based Study}

\author{G.~Sai~Pranav}
\author{Prof.~Harish J.~Palanthandalam-Madapusi}

\begin{abstract}
Can the final joint configuration of one controlled grasp identify both
the shape and size of a known object type at an unseen scale? We
investigate this question with a 16-joint LEAP Hand and a reproducible
simulation-to-hardware pipeline. Three fixed computer-aided-design
objects---a cube, sphere, and cylinder---are placed at one common pose in
MuJoCo and uniformly scaled from 50\% to 150\% in 5\% increments. During
each grasp, collision stops all four joints of a contacted finger. The
resulting 16 final joint angles train a small neural network to classify
shape and regress scale; a separate rule rejects ambiguous or distant
grasps as unknown. On 15 held-out simulated sizes, the network correctly
classified 13 (86.7\%) and achieved a 1.36\,mm mean absolute
ground-truth-dimension-weighted scale error. The rejection rule accepted
five of these 15 grasps, all correctly classified. The physical-hand
program implements the corresponding grasp using motor current as a
contact proxy, but this manuscript reports no physical-hand recognition
trials. The results establish a simulation baseline for joint-angle-only
recognition under fixed placement, while leaving real-world transfer and
rejection of genuinely novel objects to future experiments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A robot can obtain information about an object by acting on it. For a
dexterous hand, the joint configuration reached while closing around a
surface depends on both the object's geometry and its size. This suggests
a form of active perception in which the movement of the hand generates
the observation used for recognition. Motor positions are available on
the LEAP Hand without an external camera or dedicated tactile skin
\citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}.

Prior work demonstrates that hand kinematics can support recognition.
\citet{VasquezPerdereau2017} constructed proprioceptive shape signatures
from joint angles and studied recognition across object sizes and poses.
\citet{SintovMeir2023} used computationally generated grasps for
kinesthetic object recognition and evaluated recognition of both
individual instances and broad geometric categories. These studies
motivate a controlled experiment that asks whether a compact final-angle
observation can support \emph{both} shape classification and a continuous
estimate of physical scale.

The research question here is deliberately narrow: given a fixed object
mount, a fixed exploratory grasp, and a set of three trained object
classes, how well can the final 16 joint angles distinguish class and
estimate size at scales excluded from training? The fixed mount removes
object-pose variation so that the effect of scale can be examined first.
The project also implements a matching grasp and prediction program for
the physical hand. Its intended transfer mechanism replaces simulated
mesh contact with a motor-current threshold, while preserving the
four-joint finger stopping rule and final-angle input. This transfer
mechanism has not yet been evaluated with labeled real-hand trials.

The contributions of the present implementation are (i) a parameterized
MuJoCo grasp-data generator for STEP objects and a 16-joint LEAP Hand
model, (ii) a reproducible joint-angle classifier and size regressor with
an explicit unknown-output rule, and (iii) a physical-hand execution and
logging program. The measurements reported below concern the simulated
data generator and held-out scale evaluation.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Hand, software, and object models}
\label{sec:setup}

The platform is a four-finger, 16-actuator LEAP Hand
\citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}. The logical joint vector is ordered as
index joints 0--3, middle joints 4--7, ring joints 8--11, and thumb joints
12--15. All commanded and recorded joint angles in the experiment are in
degrees. MuJoCo stores joint coordinates internally in radians
\citep{TodorovErezTassa2012}. The included hand URDF and link meshes define
the simulated geometry. The URDF limits at joints 0, 8, 13, and 14 were
expanded to admit the configured grasp; no simulation-to-hardware angle
offset is currently applied.

Three STEP models are used: a cube, sphere, and cylinder. The reference
cube side length is 50\,mm. The reference radius is 25\,mm for both the
sphere and cylinder. Every object is fixed at the same pose relative
to the simulation frame: translation $(-0.060,-0.048,-0.035)$\,m and
roll--pitch--yaw orientation $(-180^\circ,0^\circ,0^\circ)$. STEP
coordinates are treated as millimetres, converted to metres, and
multiplied by a uniform scale factor. The same factor changes every
object dimension, although the model predicts only the designated size
measure in Table~\ref{tab:objects}.

\subsection{A standardized active grasp}
\label{sec:grasp}

The grasp uses the same start posture, target posture, and one-degree
command increments for every simulated object. Table~\ref{tab:grasp}
specifies the joint vectors in the logical order defined above. Joint
values are clamped at their target angles. The grasp terminates when all
four fingers have stopped, with a cap of 250 iterations. A finger stops
as one unit: contact at any of its links freezes all four joints of that
finger, while other fingers may continue closing. Reaching all configured
target angles also stops a finger.

\subsection{Simulation of contact and data collection}
\label{sec:simulation}

The simulation code imports each STEP object with CadQuery, tessellates
it into a triangle mesh, and mounts that mesh as a fixed body in a
MuJoCo scene containing the hand URDF. Explicit object--hand collision
pairs are added. Contact geometries are assigned to a finger through
their parent bodies; contact with the palm or an unexpected stationary
finger is treated as an invalid scene. Any object already touching the
hand in the initial posture is also rejected.

At each grasp iteration, the simulator advances one active finger at a
time and tests the proposed posture for mesh contact. If contact occurs,
eight bisection steps between the last clear posture and the blocked
posture locate a nearby collision-free final angle. The entire finger
then stops. This is a kinematic contact experiment: joint positions are
set directly, with no learned grasp policy, motor dynamics, force
prediction, or simulated motor current. The recorded feature for scene
$i$ is the final vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q_i=(q_{i,0},\ldots,q_{i,15})\in\mathbb R^{16},
\label{eq:angles}
\end{equation}
along with its class, scale, physical size, and each finger's stop
reason. A complete step-by-step trace is saved for replay in the
MuJoCo viewer.

The scale sweep is $s\in\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,1.50\}$, so the three classes
produce $3\times21=63$ simulated scenes. Each class has exactly one
deterministic simulated grasp per size. No object-pose randomization or
repeated physical trials are part of this dataset.

\subsection{Feature preparation and model}
\label{sec:model}

Only the final angles in Eq.~\eqref{eq:angles} are model inputs.
Positions, velocities, motor currents, fingertip trajectories, and
per-step contact histories are not training features. Each angle is
normalized by the configured grasp span:
\begin{equation}
x_j=\frac{q_j-a_j}{b_j-a_j},
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
where $a_j$ and $b_j$ are the start and target angles. For a joint with
$a_j=b_j$, the implementation substitutes a denominator of one. The
input $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^{16}$ passes through a fully connected
32-unit hyperbolic-tangent layer. The four output values consist of
three class logits and one continuous predicted scale factor
$\hat s$. A softmax converts the logits to class probabilities. With
three classes, the network has
$16(32)+32+32(4)+4=676$ trainable parameters.

Training minimizes mean class cross-entropy plus mean squared scale
error:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=
-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_{i,c_i}
+\lambda\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat s_i-s_i)^2,
\qquad \lambda=1.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The NumPy implementation uses full-batch Adam for 1000 epochs, a
learning rate of 0.02, and random seed 7. For each simulated grasp,
50 independent copies receive zero-mean Gaussian measurement noise
with a 0.5-degree standard deviation per angle; the unmodified
example is retained. The augmented copies approximate position
measurement variation but do not model systematic calibration error.

\subsection{Validation protocol and unknown decision}
\label{sec:validation}

The data split is made by \emph{size before augmentation}. Every fifth
scale---50\%, 75\%, 100\%, 125\%, and 150\%---is held out for each
class. Thus 48 distinct simulated scenes produce 2448 training
examples (48 original vectors plus 50 noisy copies each), while 15
unmodified scenes are used for validation. A separate final model is
then trained on all 63 scenes and their noisy copies (3213 examples)
for later physical-hand use. The validation figures in this paper
refer to the model trained on the 48-scene subset.

Raw classification accuracy is the fraction of the 15 validation
scenes whose highest-probability class equals the ground-truth class.
For scale, the reported mean absolute error is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{size}} =
\frac{1}{15}\sum_{i=1}^{15}
|\hat s_i-s_i|\,d_{c_i},
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
where $d_{c_i}$ is the \emph{ground-truth class's} reference dimension.
This measures scale regression in physical units even for a
misclassified scene; it is not the error of the final class-conditioned
size output when the predicted class is wrong.

The deployed output may instead be \texttt{unknown}. Four checks are
applied in order. First, the Euclidean distance from the normalized
input to its nearest simulated reference vector must not exceed a
distance threshold. That threshold is 1.5 times the largest nearest
training-scene distance of the 15 held-out scales (0.0697 for the
present dataset). Second, the largest class probability must be at
least 0.75. Third, the network class must agree with the class of
the nearest simulated reference vector. Fourth, the difference
between the two smallest \emph{classwise} nearest-reference distances,
measured in raw angle degrees, must be at least $2^\circ$. A failure
at any step returns \texttt{unknown} without a size. Otherwise, the
predicted scale is clipped to $[0.50,1.50]$ and multiplied by the
reference dimension for the predicted class.

The distance threshold was calculated from the same 15 scales on
which accepted/rejected outcomes are reported. Those outcomes are
therefore descriptive, not an independent evaluation of the
unknown-object rule. No untrained CAD object class was tested.

\subsection{Physical-hand implementation and reproducibility}
\label{sec:physical}

The hardware program sends the grasp through \texttt{ExArm}. It reads
all 16 motor positions, velocities, and signed currents through the
Dynamixel driver. For each
moving joint, a current exceeding its calibrated threshold in the
commanded direction stops that joint's entire finger at its measured
pose. The per-joint threshold vectors are
$[22,80,25,20]$ for each of the index, middle, and ring fingers and
$[25,70,25,20]$ for the thumb, in the values returned by the motor-state
reader. The hand settles for 2\,s before the grasp, waits 0.2\,s between
command steps, and aborts after repeated state-read failures or more
than 250 steps. These thresholds are configurable and may require
recalibration; motor current is a contact proxy, not a direct
measurement of contact force.

After the final state read, the physical program supplies the measured
16-angle vector to the saved model, prints class and size or an
unknown reason, and saves a timestamped JSON result. It also saves
joint-state traces and the final angle vector as CSV files. This code
path is implemented, but the repository contains no labeled
real-object results, so no sim-to-real recognition accuracy is claimed.

The experiment settings are in \path{utils/Constants.py}. The main
entry points generate and train simulated grasps
(\path{Train_Proprioception.py}), replay saved grasp trajectories
(\path{View_Proprioception.py}), and run recognition on the physical
hand (\path{Run_Proprioception.py}). The
\path{Data/Proprioception} directory holds final grasp angles, step
traces, augmented samples, validation rows, the training report, and
the saved model weights. The implementation and data form part of the
Ex-ARM project \citep{ExARM}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The generated dataset contains 63 completed simulated grasps and
5993 recorded time-step rows, including the initial posture of each
scene. Across the 252 finger endings (four per scene), 210 were
attributed to object contact and 42 to reaching the configured
angle limits. These counts confirm that the simulated closing motion
generated object contact in many finger trajectories; they are not a
measure of recognition performance.

On the 15 scales excluded from the validation model's training set,
13 raw network class predictions were correct (86.7\%). Table
\ref{tab:validation} shows results by class. Both raw errors were
sphere-or-cylinder scenes predicted as cube. The scale-factor
regression achieved the ground-truth-dimension-weighted MAE of
1.36\,mm defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mae}.

The unknown-output rule rejected 10 of the 15 held-out grasps: five
because the two nearest classes had similar angle vectors and five
because the network's maximum class probability was below 0.75.
All five accepted grasps had the correct class, but the acceptance
rate was only one third. Since the threshold was set using these
same held-out sizes and no novel object class was tested, this
result cannot establish real-world unknown-object detection.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The held-out-scale results support the limited proposition that a
single controlled closing motion can carry information about both
class and scale when the object remains at a known pose in the
simulator. At the same time, some sphere and cylinder configurations
produced final angles that the network assigned to the cube class,
and the conservative rejection checks discarded most validation
grasps. A larger set of independent test sizes, repeated grasps,
and baseline classifiers would be required to estimate robustness
and compare alternative representations.

The central transfer uncertainty lies in how a simulated collision
corresponds to a motor-current event on the physical hand. Real
motors, transmission compliance, current noise, friction, and object
mount errors can shift the final angle relative to the idealized
collision-free boundary used for training. Gaussian perturbation
of 0.5 degrees does not reproduce these effects. The experiment also
holds orientation and position fixed, so its numerical results do
not generalize to freely held or moving objects. Because each class
has one mesh and only uniform scaling, the model has not learned
within-class shape variation.

The present manuscript concerns the implemented final-angle
method. It does not use fingertip reconstruction, multi-grasp
surface mapping, or an adaptive exploration policy. Such methods
could add information where a single grasp is ambiguous, but their
benefit would need to be tested against this baseline. Similarly,
open-set performance requires independent objects that were absent
from both training and threshold selection.

A physical evaluation should next mount measured examples of all
three classes at the simulated pose and collect repeated labeled
grasps over several sizes. The comparison should report class
accuracy, size error, rejection rate, and differences between each
real final-angle vector and its matching simulated vector. Separate
tests with new shapes and perturbed placements would then test
unknown detection and domain transfer without tuning thresholds on
the test cases.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study implements active object recognition with a LEAP Hand
using only final joint angles. With a fixed object pose and 63 simulated
grasps across cube, sphere, and cylinder models, a 676-parameter
network classified 13 of 15 held-out sizes correctly and achieved
1.36\,mm mean absolute scale-regression error in the reported size
units. A rule-based unknown output accepted five held-out grasps,
all correctly classified, at low coverage. These are simulation
results. The corresponding physical current-limited grasp and
inference code are available for experimental measurement, but the
accuracy of physical recognition and the ability to reject
genuinely novel objects remain open questions.
