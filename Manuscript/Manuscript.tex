\title{Grasp to Identify: Physical-Only Proprioceptive Recognition
of Fifteen Classes with a LEAP Hand}

\author{G.~Sai~Pranav}
\author{Harish J.~Palanthandalam-Madapusi}

\begin{abstract}
Robotic hands commonly rely on vision or dedicated tactile sensors for object recognition. This study investigates whether the final joint configurations of a physical LEAP Hand can distinguish enrolled objects using proprioceptive information alone. The approach exploits differences in finger positions produced when objects interrupt controlled grasping movements. A hand with 16 actuated joints executes three programmed grips with each object positioned near a consistent palm location. Motor currents indicate resistance: when a joint exceeds its configured threshold, all four joints of that finger stop while the remaining fingers continue. The final joint angles from the three grips form a 48-dimensional feature vector; motor currents are used for control but excluded from recognition inputs. Normalised angles are fed into a neural classifier with a single hidden layer of 24 units. The dataset comprises 75 physical trials, with five trials for each of 14 objects and an empty-hand condition. Gaussian perturbations augment training data after the physical trials are divided into validation folds. In three-fold within-dataset validation, the classifier correctly identified 70 of 75 held-out trials \(93.3\%\). A decision rule combining class probabilities and distance to recorded grasps accepted 67 trials as known classes, of which 65 were correct \(97.0\%\); eight were uncertain, and none were rejected as unknown. These findings indicate that final grasp angles contain information sufficient to distinguish many enrolled items under controlled conditions. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\label{sec:introduction}

A robot can learn about an object by interacting with it. For a
dexterous hand, the joint positions reached while closing around an
object depend on the surfaces that interrupt finger motion. These
positions may therefore contain information about the object even
without an external camera or dedicated tactile skin. This use of
the hand's internal state is known as proprioceptive perception. The
LEAP Hand is a dexterous, anthropomorphic hand developed for robot
learning and manipulation \citet{ShawAgarwalPathak2023} and references therein.

Previous work has shown that joint information can support object
recognition. \citet{VasquezPerdereau2017} developed proprioceptive
shape signatures from robotic-hand joint angles and studied shape
identification across object sizes and poses. \citet{SintovMeir2023}
used grasp information for kinesthetic object recognition without
dedicated tactile sensors. More recently, \citet{Kutsuzawa2024}
investigated learning-based shape and stiffness estimation from
joint angles and velocities in a multi-fingered hand. These studies
motivate a physical-hand experiment using a simple observation:
the final joint angles of a repeatable grip sequence.

The main research question is whether final angles from the physical
LEAP Hand can distinguish different objects, including an
empty-hand condition. A second question is whether combining
repeated grip sequences might improve decisions that are uncertain
after one sequence; the current dataset evaluates individual
sequences only. The model predicts a class label, rather than object
size. Each object label represents the recorded physical exemplar;
recognition of another instance of the same kind is a separate
generalization question.

The implementation provides repeatable grips, a resumable
physical-data collection procedure, a neural classifier, a
nearest-trial comparison method, and an interface for running the
hand and viewing the results. Both models use only physical grasp
measurements for training and recognition.

Figure~\ref{fig:grasp} shows the physical hand during a bottle grasp.

\section{Experimental Setup and Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Physical hand and controlled grips}
\label{sec:grasp}

The protocol uses a four-fingered LEAP Hand with 16 actuated joints,
four per finger \citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}. The fingers are the
index, middle, ring, and thumb. All angles are expressed in degrees.
Each object is placed near a consistent point relative to the palm,
and every object receives the same programmed grip sequence.
The saved trial files do not record measured placement offsets or
object orientation. Placement is therefore a condition of the
collection procedure rather than a property inferred by the model.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{Figures/hand_grasping_bottle.jpg}
\caption{The physical LEAP Hand grasping the water bottle used in
the study. The fingers contact the object while the hand closes
under motor-current stopping rules.}
\label{fig:grasp}
\end{figure}

For each configured grip, the hand moves to that grip's starting
posture. Its 16 joints then advance toward specified targets using
the per-joint step sizes in the experiment settings. During closure,
motor current served as an indicator of resistance. If a moving
joint's signed current exceeds its configured threshold, all four
joints of that finger stop at their measured positions. The
remaining fingers continue until resistance or an angle target
stops them. A grip ends when all four fingers have stopped and
is aborted after at most 250 iterations. The hand moves to the
next grip's starting posture before continuing the sequence.

After each grip, the program reads its final 16 joint angles,
retrying for up to 10 seconds if position reporting is temporarily
unavailable. The final vectors are concatenated in grip order.
The hand runs three distinct grips, yielding 48 input angles
per physical trial.

During collection, the final angles and time-series log are saved
before the hand returns to its starting posture. Motor currents
control the movement but are not classifier inputs. No camera
images or tactile arrays are used as recognition features.

\subsection{Physical dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

Each training record consists of an enrolled class label and
one concatenated final-angle vector,
\[
\mathbf{q}_i=
\left[\mathbf{q}_i^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{q}_i^{(G)}\right]
\in\mathbb{R}^{16G},
\]
where \(G\) is the number of configured grips and each
\(\mathbf{q}_i^{(g)}\) contains the 16 final joint angles
from grip \(g\).
The data-collection program stores every completed trial
with its final angles and time-series log. It also saves a
collection plan before recording begins, so interrupted
sessions can resume without discarding earlier trials.
Additional trials and new classes can be added later. A mistaken
trial may be retaken; the previous record is archived after a
complete replacement has been checked.

The dataset has 15 labels: water bottle, Rubik's cube, ball,
bowl, mug, basic cylinder, power bank, spectacles case, mouse,
stapler, pencil box, Pyraminx, tape measure, spice bottle, and
no object. Fourteen labels identify physical items and one
identifies an empty-hand grasp. Five recorded trials per label
give 75 physical trials. The trial files contain the class name,
final angles, and time-series motor log; they do not contain
measured object dimensions or placement coordinates. The
analyzed class set was selected after preliminary analysis
of recorded grasps rather than fixed before data collection.

\subsection{Neural network and training}
\label{sec:model}

The neural network receives only the \(16G\) final joint
angles. Each angle is normalized by the start and target
angles of its grip and joint:
\[
x_{g,j}=\frac{q_{g,j}-a_{g,j}}{b_{g,j}-a_{g,j}},
\]
where \(a_{g,j}\) and \(b_{g,j}\) are the start and target
angles for joint \(j\) in grip \(g\). A joint with equal start and
target values is assigned a normalized value of zero.

The classifier has \(16G\) input units, one fully connected
24-unit hidden layer with a hyperbolic tangent activation,
and one output for each enrolled class. A softmax
operation converts the outputs to class probabilities.
For \(C\) classes, the model has \(384G+24+25C\) trainable
parameters. With three grips and 15 classes, it has
1,551 parameters. The model does not
predict object size.

Training uses full-batch Adam optimization with a learning
rate of 0.01, weight decay of 0.001, and a limit of 500
epochs. Each real trial is retained and supplemented
with 20 noisy copies during training. Independent
zero-mean Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of
\(0.5^\circ\) is added to each angle. A second
\(0.5^\circ\) Gaussian perturbation is shared by the
four joints of each finger within each grip. These artificial copies
approximate small measurement and grasp variation; they
are not counted as additional physical trials. Samples
are weighted inversely to the number of real trials in
their class to reduce the effect of unequal class sizes.

\subsection{Validation and recognition decisions}
\label{sec:validation}

The implemented evaluation uses up to three folds, with
real trials from every class assigned to different folds
before noise is added. Noise augmentation is performed
only on each training fold. The validation fold therefore
contains measurements from the physical hand, not
perturbed copies of training samples. The best epoch in
each fold is selected by validation cross-entropy, with
training stopped after 80 epochs without improvement.
The final model is then trained on all real trials for
the median number of selected epochs.

The class probabilities are adjusted with a temperature
selected from the held-out folds. We also compute the
distance from a new normalized angle vector to the
nearest saved physical training grasp. A prediction is
reported as \emph{unknown} when this distance exceeds
1.5 times the 95th percentile of the validation
distances. Otherwise, it is reported as \emph{uncertain}
when the largest class probability is below 0.75 or
the difference between the two largest probabilities
is below 0.20. A known class is returned only when
these checks pass. These thresholds are calibrated from
known-class validation trials. Their ability to reject
genuinely unseen objects requires a separate evaluation.

If a neural prediction remains uncertain, the program
can request another trial of the same object, up to
three trials. Each trial runs the configured grip sequence.
The class probabilities are averaged across trials, while
the nearest-reference distances are combined using their
median. The resulting decision is saved with each trial.
A separate nearest-trial method assigns the class of the saved
physical grasp with the smallest Euclidean distance
in degrees. That method supplies a comparison baseline;
it does not produce probabilities or an unknown-object
decision.

\subsection{Software implementation}
\label{sec:software}

The grip and training programs are written in python.
 The terminal interface collects,
resumes, extends, and retakes physical trials. It also
trains both models and runs the hand for recognition.
The presentation window evaluates both trained models
from the same recognition grasp. It shows both decisions,
the neural probabilities or nearest-trial distances,
and a visualization driven by the measured hand pose.
The classifier itself uses only recorded physical angles.
The repository retains the trial CSV files and model
reports, including the neural confusion matrices and
nearest-trial evaluation results.

\subsection{Interpretation and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The internal results support the presence of class information
in the final joint configuration. They do not establish
performance on a different exemplar
of a named object, or an object absent from training.

The 15-class set was selected after preliminary analysis
of the recorded grasps, the probability temperature and distance
threshold were chosen from the held-out folds. Accepted
accuracy on those folds may be optimistic for new data.
Only five trials are available for each label; a single
error changes that label's measured recall by 20
percentage points.

The final joint configuration is not a unique description
of an object's shape. Items of different appearance may
produce similar angles at the same palm location. Gaussian
angle perturbations do not create independent physical
grasps and cannot reproduce every change in placement,
friction, compliance, or motor-current response. The
near-overlap of the water-bottle and mug profiles in
Figure~\ref{fig:angles} illustrates this ambiguity within the
enrolled set. The
software permits up to three grip sequences for an
uncertain recognition and averages neural probabilities,
but the saved validation reports evaluate individual
sequences; they do not measure the benefit of repetition.
A future independent test should fix the class set,
model, and thresholds before collecting labeled grasps
in later sessions, include new object instances, and
reserve some entire object classes to measure unknown
rejection.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This work demonstrates physical-only proprioceptive
recognition with a LEAP Hand using three sequential
grips and a 48-angle neural input. In internal three-fold
validation, the 15-class model classified 70/75 trials
correctly and accepted 67/75 as known-class decisions.
Of the accepted decisions, 65 were correct.
The nearest-trial baseline provided a separate
leave-one-trial-out comparison. These measurements show
that final grasp angles distinguish many enrolled items
under the recorded conditions. Independent sessions,
new physical instances, unseen objects, and the effect
of repeat grasps remain to be tested.
