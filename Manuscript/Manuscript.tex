\title{Design of a Physical-Only Proprioceptive Recognition System
for Twenty Household Objects with a LEAP Hand}

\author{G.~Sai~Pranav}
\author{Harish J.~Palanthandalam-Madapusi}

\begin{abstract}
Robotic hands commonly use vision or dedicated tactile sensors to
recognize objects. This study examines whether a physical LEAP Hand
can identify household items using only the joint angles reached
after controlled grasps. Motor-current thresholds stop an entire
finger when resistance is detected. Final angles from an ordered
sequence of grips form the input to a compact neural classifier;
all training measurements come from the physical hand. The system
is designed for 20 household-object classes, with three sequential
grips providing 48 final joint-angle measurements per trial. A
24-unit hidden layer maps these measurements to class probabilities.
Physical trials are augmented only after real measurements have
been separated into training and validation folds. A distance and
confidence rule permits uncertain or unknown outputs, and repeated
trials can be combined when one reading is inconclusive. Evaluation
requires held-out trials across objects and sessions, as well as
objects excluded from training to test unknown-object rejection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\label{sec:introduction}

A robot can learn about an object by interacting with it. For a
dexterous hand, the joint positions reached while closing around an
object depend on the surfaces that interrupt finger motion. These
positions may therefore contain information about the object even
without an external camera or dedicated tactile skin. This use of
the hand's internal state is known as proprioceptive perception. The
LEAP Hand is a dexterous, anthropomorphic hand developed for robot
learning and manipulation \citet{ShawAgarwalPathak2023} and references therein.

Previous work has shown that joint information can support object
recognition. \citet{VasquezPerdereau2017} developed proprioceptive
shape signatures from robotic-hand joint angles and studied shape
identification across object sizes and poses. \citet{SintovMeir2023}
used grasp information for kinesthetic object recognition without
dedicated tactile sensors. More recently, \citet{Kutsuzawa2024}
investigated learning-based shape and stiffness estimation from
joint angles and velocities in a multi-fingered hand. These studies
motivate a physical-hand experiment using a simple observation:
the final joint angles of a repeatable grip sequence.

The main research question is whether final angles from the physical
LEAP Hand can distinguish 20 enrolled household objects. A second
question is whether combining repeated grip sequences improves
decisions that are uncertain after one sequence.
The model predicts object identity, rather than object size. Unless
multiple examples of a household category are collected, each label
refers to one physical item; recognition of another item of the same
kind is a separate generalisation question.

The implementation provides repeatable grips, a resumable
physical-data collection procedure, a neural classifier, a
nearest-trial comparison method, and an interface for running the
hand and viewing the results. The study is organized around
recognition among 20 enrolled household objects.

\section{Experimental Setup and Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Physical hand and controlled grips}
\label{sec:grasp}

The protocol uses a four-fingered LEAP Hand with 16 actuated joints
\citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}. with 4 joints per finger, and four fingers. Namely, Index, Middle, Ring, and Thumb. 
All angles are expressed in degrees. Each
object is placed near a consistent point relative to the palm,
with a prescribed variation of 2~cm in position and
10$^\circ$ in orientation. Every object receives the same
programmed grip sequence. Placement is a controlled experimental
condition rather than a property inferred by the model.

For each configured grip, the hand moves to that grip's starting
posture. Its 16 joints then advance toward specified targets using
the per-joint step sizes in the experiment settings. During closure,
motor current served as an indicator of resistance. If a moving
joint's signed current exceeds its configured threshold, all four
joints of that finger stop at their measured positions. The
remaining fingers continue until resistance or an angle target
stops them. A grip ends when all four fingers have stopped and
is aborted after at most 250 iterations. The hand moves to the
next grip's starting posture before continuing the sequence.

After each grip, the program reads its final 16 joint angles,
retrying for up to 10 seconds if position reporting is temporarily
unavailable. The final vectors are concatenated in grip order.
The hand runs three distinct grips for different situations, yielding 48 input angles
per physical trial.

During collection, the final angles and time-series log are saved
before the hand returns to its starting posture. Motor currents
control the movement but are not classifier inputs. No camera
images or tactile arrays are used as recognition features.

\subsection{Twenty-object physical dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

Each training record consists of a household-object label and
one concatenated final-angle vector,
\[
\mathbf{q}_i=
\left[\mathbf{q}_i^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{q}_i^{(G)}\right]
\in\mathbb{R}^{16G},
\]
where \(G\) is the number of configured grips and each
\(\mathbf{q}_i^{(g)}\) contains the 16 final joint angles
from grip \(g\).
The data-collection program stores every completed trial
with its final angles and time-series log. It also saves a
collection plan before recording begins, so interrupted
sessions can resume without discarding earlier trials.
Additional trials and new classes can be added later. A mistaken
trial may be retaken; the previous record is archived after a
complete replacement has been checked.

The study design specifies 20 distinct household
objects, such as a water bottle, a bowl, a ball, a spectacles case, a ceramic mug, etc.
the model is trained with ten independent physical trials
per object, or 200 labeled trials. The selected objects are
graspable within the hand's configured motion limits and
vary in geometry and size. Their names, dimensions,
and placement orientation are recorded with the
dataset. Repeated trials include reseating the object
between measurements, varying their position and orientation.

\subsection{Neural network and training}
\label{sec:model}

The neural network receives only the \(16G\) final joint
angles. Each angle is normalized by the start and target
angles of its grip and joint:
\[
x_{g,j}=\frac{q_{g,j}-a_{g,j}}{b_{g,j}-a_{g,j}},
\]
where \(a_{g,j}\) and \(b_{g,j}\) are the start and target
angles for joint \(j\) in grip \(g\). A joint with equal start and
target values is assigned a normalized value of zero.

The classifier has \(16G\) input units, one fully connected
24-unit hidden layer with a hyperbolic tangent activation,
and one output for each enrolled object. A softmax
operation converts the outputs to class probabilities.
For \(C\) classes, the model has \(384G+24+25C\) trainable
parameters. With three grips and 20 classes, it has
1,676 parameters. The model does not predict object size.

Training uses full-batch Adam optimization with a learning
rate of 0.01, weight decay of 0.001, and a limit of 500
epochs. Each real trial is retained and supplemented
with 20 noisy copies during training. Independent
zero-mean Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of
\(0.5^\circ\) is added to each angle. A second
\(0.5^\circ\) Gaussian perturbation is shared by the
four joints of each finger within each grip. These artificial copies
approximate small measurement and grasp variation; they
are not counted as additional physical trials. Samples
are weighted inversely to the number of real trials in
their class to reduce the effect of unequal class sizes.

\subsection{Validation and recognition decisions}
\label{sec:validation}

The implemented evaluation uses up to three folds, with
real trials from every class assigned to different folds
before noise is added. Noise augmentation is performed
only on each training fold. The validation fold therefore
contains measurements from the physical hand, not
perturbed copies of training samples. The best epoch in
each fold is selected by validation cross-entropy, with
training stopped after 80 epochs without improvement.
The final model is then trained on all real trials for
the median number of selected epochs.

The class probabilities are adjusted with a temperature
selected from the held-out folds. We also compute the
distance from a new normalized angle vector to the
nearest saved physical training grasp. A prediction is
reported as \emph{unknown} when this distance exceeds
1.5 times the 95th percentile of the validation
distances. Otherwise, it is reported as \emph{uncertain}
when the largest class probability is below 0.75 or
the difference between the two largest probabilities
is below 0.20. A known class is returned only when
these checks pass. These thresholds are calibrated from
known-class validation trials. Their ability to reject
genuinely unseen objects requires a separate evaluation.

If a neural prediction remains uncertain, the program
can request another trial of the same object, up to
three trials. Each trial runs the configured grip sequence.
The class probabilities are averaged across trials, while
the nearest-reference distances are combined using their
median. The resulting decision is saved with each trial.
A separate nearest-trial method assigns the class of the saved
physical grasp with the smallest Euclidean distance
in degrees. That method supplies a comparison baseline;
it does not produce probabilities or an unknown-object
decision.

\subsection{Software implementation}
\label{sec:software}

The grip and physical-only training programs are part
of Ex-ARM \citep{ExARM}. The terminal interface can
collect, resume, extend, and retake physical trials,
train either model, and run the hand for recognition.
The presentation window shows the final decision,
the neural probabilities for every enrolled class,
and a visualization driven by the measured hand pose.
The classifier itself uses only recorded physical angles.

\section{Evaluation Protocol and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Twenty-object evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The principal outcome is raw classification accuracy across
the 20 enrolled household objects. Each held-out physical
trial contributes one true class and one predicted class.
A 20-by-20 confusion matrix and per-class recall identify
object pairs that produce similar final joint postures.
Macro-averaged recall is included alongside overall
accuracy so that classes with fewer trials do not disappear
inside an aggregate score.

The decision rule introduces two additional outcomes:
\emph{uncertain} and \emph{unknown}. Evaluation therefore
reports coverage, defined as the fraction of trials
assigned a known class, and accuracy among accepted
predictions. These measures are reported together;
a high accepted accuracy by itself could result from
rejecting many difficult trials. To assess unknown-object
rejection, some physical objects must be excluded entirely
from model training and treated as novel at test time.

A later collection session should be held out until the
network, normalization, and decision thresholds are fixed.
This test measures how object placement, motor calibration,
and hand state affect recognition beyond the within-session
validation folds. The same held-out objects can then be
classified after one grip sequence and after repeated
sequences. This paired comparison measures whether another
physical observation improves the decision.

\subsection{Interpretation and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The final joint configuration contains information about
where the fingers were stopped, but it is not a unique
description of an object's shape. Items of different
appearance may produce similar joint angles when held at
the same palm location. The use of several grip patterns
may expose more of the object, while also introducing
additional variation if the object shifts between grips.
These effects must be evaluated on recorded physical
trials, rather than inferred from the network architecture.

Gaussian angle perturbations can help tolerate small
measurement changes but do not create new independent
physical grasps. They cannot reproduce every change in
placement, friction, compliance, or motor-current response.
The study therefore relies on repeated real trials and
later-session testing. If each class is represented by
only one physical exemplar, recognition of another item
from the same category remains a separate question.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work specifies a physical-only proprioceptive
recognition system for 20 household objects using a
LEAP Hand. Current thresholds stop contacted fingers,
and the final angles from three sequential grips form
a 48-value input to a compact neural classifier.
The software supports resumable collection, trial
retakes, class probabilities, uncertain or unknown
decisions, and repeated recognition attempts.

The evaluation protocol includes held-out physical
accuracy, per-class performance, decision coverage, and
tests on later sessions and unseen objects.
