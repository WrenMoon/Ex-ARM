\title{Toward Proprioceptive Recognition of Twenty Household Objects with a LEAP Hand}

\author{G.~Sai~Pranav}
\author{Harish J.~Palanthandalam-Madapusi}

\begin{abstract}
Robotic hands commonly use vision or dedicated tactile sensors to
recognize objects. This study investigates whether the final joint
configuration of a physical LEAP Hand can instead identify household
objects after a controlled grasp. The proposed experiment comprises
20 labeled household objects, with repeated real-hand grasps for each
object. Motor current indicates contact and stops the entire contacted
finger. The resulting 16 joint angles are the only inputs to a small
neural classifier. Training uses physical trials alone, with small
angle perturbations applied after splitting the real trials for
validation. Repeated grasps can be combined when a single prediction
is uncertain. The available pilot dataset contains three geometric
objects with three physical trials each. In three-fold held-out-trial
validation, the neural model classified all nine trials correctly.
A separate four-object household pilot produced 11 correct predictions
in 12 leave-one-trial-out tests using a nearest-trial baseline; a
household neural model has not yet been trained. These results support
the feasibility of collecting and classifying physical grasp data,
but do not establish accuracy for the planned 20-object study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\label{sec:introduction}

A robot can learn about an object by interacting with it. For a
dexterous hand, the joint positions reached while closing around an
object depend on the surfaces that interrupt finger motion. These
positions may therefore contain information about the object even
without an external camera or dedicated tactile skin. This use of
the hand's internal state is known as proprioceptive perception. The
LEAP Hand is a dexterous, anthropomorphic hand developed for robot
learning and manipulation \citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}.

Previous work has shown that joint information can support object
recognition. \citet{VasquezPerdereau2017} developed proprioceptive
shape signatures from robotic-hand joint angles and studied shape
identification across object sizes and poses. \citet{SintovMeir2023}
used grasp information for kinesthetic object recognition without
dedicated tactile sensors. More recently, \citet{Kutsuzawa2024}
investigated learning-based shape and stiffness estimation from
joint angles and velocities in a multi-fingered hand. These studies
motivate a physical-hand experiment using a deliberately simple
observation: the final joint angles of one standardized grasp.

The main research question is whether this 16-angle observation can
distinguish 20 enrolled household objects when all data are collected
with the physical LEAP Hand. A second question is whether combining
repeated grasps improves decisions that are uncertain after one grasp.
The model predicts object identity, rather than object size. Unless
multiple examples of a household category are collected, each label
refers to one physical item; recognition of another item of the same
kind is a separate generalisation question.

The present implementation provides a repeatable grasp, a resumable
physical-data collection procedure, a neural classifier, a
nearest-trial comparison method, and an interface for running the
hand and viewing the results. The available physical trials permit a
preliminary evaluation, while the larger 20-object experiment remains
to be completed. The pilot findings are reported separately from the
proposed full experiment.

\section{Experimental Setup and Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Physical hand and exploratory grasp}
\label{sec:grasp}

We used a four-fingered LEAP Hand with 16 actuated joints
\citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}. The recorded joint vector was
ordered as index joints 0--3, middle joints 4--7, ring joints
8--11, and thumb joints 12--15. All recorded angles were
expressed in degrees. Objects were presented at a consistent
position relative to the palm, and the same programmed grasp
was used for every object. This controlled placement is an
experimental condition rather than a property of the model.

The hand first moved to a configured open posture. Each joint
then advanced towards a specified final angle in one-degree
steps. During closure, motor current served as an indicator
of resistance from contact. If the measured current of any
joint in a finger exceeded its configured threshold in the
direction of motion, all four joints of that finger stopped
at their measured positions. The other fingers continued
until they encountered resistance or reached their target
angles. A run ended when all four fingers had stopped;
it was aborted if it exceeded 250 iterations.

After closure, the program read the final 16 joint angles.
A temporary loss of position reporting was handled by
retrying the final read for up to 10 seconds. During data
collection, the angles and full grasp log were saved before
the hand returned to its open posture. Motor currents were
used to control the grasp; they were not passed to the
classifier. No camera images, tactile arrays, simulated
contacts, or object meshes were used to train the
physical-only model.

\subsection{Physical dataset and proposed 20-object experiment}
\label{sec:dataset}

Each training record consists of a household-object label
and one final angle vector,
\[
\mathbf{q}_i=(q_{i0},q_{i1},\ldots,q_{i15})\in\mathbb{R}^{16}.
\]
The data-collection program stores every completed trial
with its final angles and time-series log. It also saves a
collection plan before recording begins, so interrupted
sessions can resume without discarding earlier trials.
Additional trials and new classes can be added later.

The intended full study contains 20 distinct household
objects, with a target of ten physical grasps per object,
or 200 labeled trials. The selected objects should be
graspable within the hand's configured motion limits and
should vary in geometry and size. Their names, dimensions,
and placement orientation will be recorded with the
dataset. Repeated grasps should include natural variation
in reseating the same object. A separate later session
should be reserved to test changes in placement and hand
state. These 20-object trials have not yet been collected
in the repository examined for this manuscript.

Two smaller physical datasets are currently available.
The first has three trials each of a 7.5~cm side-length
cube, a 7.5~cm diameter sphere, and a 5.0~cm diameter
cylinder. The second has three trials each of a ball,
bottle, bowl, and computer mouse. The first dataset is
used below to examine the neural model; the second
provides a household-object nearest-trial baseline.
These datasets do not contain a trained 20-class model.

\subsection{Neural network and training}
\label{sec:model}

The neural network receives only the final 16 joint
angles. Before training, each angle is normalized by
the configured start and target angles:
\[
x_j=\frac{q_j-a_j}{b_j-a_j},
\]
where \(a_j\) and \(b_j\) are the start and target
angles for joint \(j\). A joint with equal start and
target values is assigned a normalized value of zero.

The classifier has 16 input units, one fully connected
24-unit hidden layer with a hyperbolic tangent activation,
and one output for each enrolled object. A softmax
operation converts the outputs to class probabilities.
For \(C\) classes, the model has \(408+25C\) trainable
parameters. Thus, the planned 20-class model would have
908 parameters. It does not predict object size.

We used full-batch Adam optimization with a learning
rate of 0.01, weight decay of 0.001, and a limit of 500
epochs. Each real grasp was retained and supplemented
with 20 noisy copies during training. Independent
zero-mean Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of
\(0.5^\circ\) was added to each joint. A second
\(0.5^\circ\) Gaussian perturbation was shared by the
four joints of each finger. These artificial copies
approximate small measurement and grasp variation; they
are not counted as additional physical trials. Samples
were weighted inversely to the number of real trials in
their class to reduce the effect of unequal class sizes.

\subsection{Validation and recognition decisions}
\label{sec:validation}

The implemented evaluation uses up to three folds, with
real trials from every class assigned to different folds
before noise is added. Noise augmentation is performed
only on each training fold. The validation fold therefore
contains measurements from the physical hand, not
perturbed copies of training samples. The best epoch in
each fold is selected by validation cross-entropy, with
training stopped after 80 epochs without improvement.
The final model is then trained on all real trials for
the median number of selected epochs.

The class probabilities are adjusted with a temperature
selected from the held-out folds. We also compute the
distance from a new normalized angle vector to the
nearest saved physical training grasp. A prediction is
reported as \emph{unknown} when this distance exceeds
1.5 times the 95th percentile of the validation
distances. Otherwise, it is reported as \emph{uncertain}
when the largest class probability is below 0.75 or
the difference between the two largest probabilities
is below 0.20. A known class is returned only when
these checks pass. These thresholds were chosen using
known-class trials; their ability to reject genuinely
unseen objects has not yet been evaluated.

If a neural prediction remains uncertain, the program
can request another grasp of the same object, up to
three grasps. The class probabilities are averaged
across grasps, while the nearest-trial distances are
combined using their median. The resulting decision
is saved along with each individual grasp. A separate
nearest-trial method assigns the class of the saved
physical grasp with the smallest Euclidean distance
in degrees. That method supplies a comparison baseline;
it does not produce probabilities or an unknown-object
decision.

For the completed 20-object study, the primary outcome
will be raw class accuracy on held-out physical trials,
reported with a confusion matrix and per-class results.
We will also report the fraction of predictions accepted
by the decision rule, the accuracy among accepted
predictions, and the effect of repeated grasps. Trials
from later sessions and separate objects excluded from
training are needed to evaluate robustness and
unknown-object rejection independently. The current
folded evaluation does not substitute for those tests.

\subsection{Software implementation}
\label{sec:software}

The grasp and physical-only training programs are part
of Ex-ARM \citep{ExARM}. The same interface can collect
or extend a dataset, train either model, and run the
physical hand for recognition. A presentation window
shows the final decision and the neural probabilities
for every enrolled class. Its side panel visualizes
the measured hand pose using a MuJoCo rendering of the
hand \citep{TodorovErezTassa2012}. This rendering is
used for display only; the physical-only classifier
is trained from measured hand angles.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Available physical pilot results}
\label{sec:pilot_results}

Table~\ref{tab:pilot} summarises the physical
measurements available at the time of writing. In the
geometric-object pilot, the neural classifier correctly
classified all nine held-out real trials in three-fold
validation. Each class contributed three trials, and
checks. This acceptance count is descriptive because
the distance and probability settings were selected
from the same folded validation data. The final neural
model was subsequently trained on all nine physical
trials.

The separate household-object dataset contains a ball,
bottle, bowl, and computer mouse, with three trials per
object. The nearest-trial method classified 11 of the
12 trials correctly when each trial was removed in turn
from the training set. One ball trial was assigned to
the bottle class. This household result belongs to the
nearest-trial baseline; no neural model has yet been
trained or evaluated on this dataset.

The geometric pilot demonstrates that its three
particular objects produced distinguishable joint-angle
patterns across the recorded grasps. The household
baseline suggests that a similar physical-data
collection process can be used for everyday objects.
Neither result measures the accuracy of a neural
classifier trained on 20 household objects. All
training and validation trials in each pilot involve
the same physical exemplars, with only three recorded
grasps per class.

\subsection{Implications for the 20-object study}
\label{sec:discussion}

The larger study asks a more demanding question than
either pilot. As additional objects are enrolled,
different items may lead to similar final joint
configurations. A water bottle and another cylindrical
container, for example, might stop the fingers at
similar angles when placed at the same point. The
classifier must then rely on the small differences
that remain in the 16-angle vector. The planned
confusion matrix and per-class results will identify
which objects are difficult to distinguish.

The current neural training procedure perturbs
recorded angles after the training and validation
trials are separated. This protects the held-out
measurements from direct duplication, but artificial
noise cannot represent every source of physical
variation. Changes in object placement, motor
calibration, friction, compliance, and contact force
may alter the final angles in ways that are not
equivalent to small Gaussian perturbations. Collecting
additional real grasps across sessions is therefore
important for the intended 20-object experiment.

The confidence and distance rules can decline to
assign a known label, and repeated grasps can
combine several observations of the same item.
However, the reported pilot tests contain only
trained classes. The thresholds were selected from
known-class validation data. A high class
probability therefore does not establish that an
unseen object will be rejected correctly. Objects
excluded from all training, together with independent
placement and session tests, are required to measure
the behavior of the \emph{unknown} and \emph{uncertain}
outputs.

The work differs from the earlier simulation-based
shape-and-size approach in the Ex-ARM project. Here,
the neural network is trained only on measurements
from the physical hand and predicts the identity of
an enrolled object. It makes no continuous size
estimate. This choice avoids assuming that simulated
contact angles match the angles reached when real
motors exert force before their current thresholds
are exceeded. The resulting model must instead obtain
its variation from repeated physical grasps.

Prior studies have demonstrated the value of
proprioceptive information for object recognition
and property estimation
\citep{VasquezPerdereau2017,SintovMeir2023,Kutsuzawa2024}.
The present implementation contributes a compact
physical-hand classifier and a repeatable procedure
for gathering, extending, and evaluating a labeled
grasp dataset. Its present evidence is preliminary.
The 20-object study is needed before claims about
household-object recognition accuracy can be made.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study describes a physical-only proprioceptive
recognition system for the LEAP Hand. A standardized
grasp stops each finger when motor current indicates
contact, and a small neural network classifies an
object from the final 16 joint angles. The proposed
experiment will enroll 20 household objects and use
repeated physical grasps to evaluate class accuracy,
uncertain decisions, and the value of combining
multiple grasps.

The available three-object neural pilot classified
nine of nine held-out physical trials correctly.
A separate four-object household nearest-trial
baseline classified 11 of 12 trials correctly.
These results establish a starting point for the
larger physical study, but do not establish the
performance of a 20-class neural model. Completing
the 20-object dataset and testing later sessions,
placement variation, and objects withheld from
training will be necessary to assess reliability
and unknown-object rejection.
