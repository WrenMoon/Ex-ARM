\title{Physical-Only Proprioceptive Recognition of Household Objects
with a LEAP Hand: A Four-Class Pilot Toward Twenty Classes}

\author{G.~Sai~Pranav}
\author{Harish J.~Palanthandalam-Madapusi}

\begin{abstract}
Robotic hands commonly use vision or dedicated tactile sensors to
recognize objects. This study examines whether a physical LEAP Hand
can identify household items using only the joint angles reached
after controlled grasps. Motor-current thresholds stop an entire
finger when resistance is detected. Final angles from an ordered
sequence of grips form the input to a compact neural classifier;
all training measurements come from the physical hand. The planned
study will enroll 20 household objects. The available household
pilot contains a ball, bottle, bowl, and computer mouse, with three
real trials per class. Its earlier single-grip neural model correctly
classified 11 of 12 held-out trials in three-fold validation
(91.7\%). Ten predictions passed its confidence and distance rules,
and all ten were correct. A nearest-trial baseline also scored 11/12.
The current software supports three sequential grips, but no
three-grip or 20-class performance has yet been measured. These
pilot results demonstrate recognition among the four recorded items;
the broader accuracy and robustness questions remain open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\label{sec:introduction}

A robot can learn about an object by interacting with it. For a
dexterous hand, the joint positions reached while closing around an
object depend on the surfaces that interrupt finger motion. These
positions may therefore contain information about the object even
without an external camera or dedicated tactile skin. This use of
the hand's internal state is known as proprioceptive perception. The
LEAP Hand is a dexterous, anthropomorphic hand developed for robot
learning and manipulation \citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}.

Previous work has shown that joint information can support object
recognition. \citet{VasquezPerdereau2017} developed proprioceptive
shape signatures from robotic-hand joint angles and studied shape
identification across object sizes and poses. \citet{SintovMeir2023}
used grasp information for kinesthetic object recognition without
dedicated tactile sensors. More recently, \citet{Kutsuzawa2024}
investigated learning-based shape and stiffness estimation from
joint angles and velocities in a multi-fingered hand. These studies
motivate a physical-hand experiment using a simple observation:
the final joint angles of a repeatable grip sequence.

The main research question is whether final angles from the physical
LEAP Hand can distinguish 20 enrolled household objects. A second
question is whether combining repeated grip sequences improves
decisions that are uncertain after one sequence.
The model predicts object identity, rather than object size. Unless
multiple examples of a household category are collected, each label
refers to one physical item; recognition of another item of the same
kind is a separate generalisation question.

The present implementation provides repeatable grips, a resumable
physical-data collection procedure, a neural classifier, a
nearest-trial comparison method, and an interface for running the
hand and viewing the results. The available physical trials permit a
preliminary four-object evaluation. The planned 20-object experiment
has not yet been completed, so its performance is not inferred from
the pilot.

\section{Experimental Setup and Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Physical hand and controlled grips}
\label{sec:grasp}

We used a four-fingered LEAP Hand with 16 actuated joints
\citep{ShawAgarwalPathak2023}. The recorded joint vector was
ordered as index joints 0--3, middle joints 4--7, ring joints
8--11, and thumb joints 12--15. All recorded angles were
expressed in degrees. Objects were presented at a consistent
position relative to the palm, with 2cm variation in position and 10 degree variation in orientation,
 and the same programmed grip
sequence was used for every object. This controlled placement is an
experimental condition rather than a property of the model.

For each configured grip, the hand moved to that grip's starting
posture. Its 16 joints then advanced toward specified targets using
the per-joint step sizes in the experiment settings. During closure,
motor current served as an indicator of resistance. If a moving
joint's signed current exceeded its configured threshold, all four
joints of that finger stopped at their measured positions. The
remaining fingers continued until resistance or an angle target
stopped them. A grip ended when all four fingers had stopped and
was aborted after at most 250 iterations. The hand moved to the
next grip's starting posture before continuing the sequence.

After each grip, the program read its final 16 joint angles,
retrying for up to 10 seconds if position reporting was temporarily
unavailable. The final vectors were concatenated in grip order.
The current settings contain three grips, yielding 48 input angles
per physical trial. 

During collection, the final angles and time-series log were saved
before the hand returned to its starting posture. Motor currents
controlled the movement but were not classifier inputs. No camera
images or tactile arrays were used as recognition features.

\subsection{Physical dataset and proposed 20-object experiment}
\label{sec:dataset}

Each training record consists of a household-object label and
one concatenated final-angle vector,
\[
\mathbf{q}_i=
\left[\mathbf{q}_i^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{q}_i^{(G)}\right]
\in\mathbb{R}^{16G},
\]
where \(G\) is the number of configured grips and each
\(\mathbf{q}_i^{(g)}\) contains the 16 final joint angles
from grip \(g\).
The data-collection program stores every completed trial
with its final angles and time-series log. It also saves a
collection plan before recording begins, so interrupted
sessions can resume without discarding earlier trials.
Additional trials and new classes can be added later. A mistaken
trial may be retaken; the previous record is archived after a
complete replacement has been checked.

The intended full study contains 20 distinct household
objects, with a target of five to ten independent physical trials
per object, or 100--200 labeled trials. The selected objects should be
graspable within the hand's configured motion limits and
should vary in geometry and size. Their names, dimensions,
and placement orientation will be recorded with the
dataset. Repeated grasps should include natural variation
in reseating the same object. A separate later session
should be reserved to test changes in placement and hand
state. 

The available household pilot has three trials each of a ball,
bottle, bowl, and computer mouse. Both the neural classifier and
nearest-trial baseline were evaluated on these 12 measurements.
All trials came from physical objects and used the earlier
single-grip configuration. 

\subsection{Neural network and training}
\label{sec:model}

The neural network receives only the \(16G\) final joint
angles. Each angle is normalized by the start and target
angles of its grip and joint:
\[
x_{g,j}=\frac{q_{g,j}-a_{g,j}}{b_{g,j}-a_{g,j}},
\]
where \(a_{g,j}\) and \(b_{g,j}\) are the start and target
angles for joint \(j\) in grip \(g\). A joint with equal start and
target values is assigned a normalized value of zero.

The classifier has \(16G\) input units, one fully connected
24-unit hidden layer with a hyperbolic tangent activation,
and one output for each enrolled object. A softmax
operation converts the outputs to class probabilities.
For \(C\) classes, the model has \(384G+24+25C\) trainable
parameters. The current three-grip, 20-class design would have
1,676 parameters; the historical single-grip, four-class pilot
had 508. The model does not predict object size.

We used full-batch Adam optimization with a learning
rate of 0.01, weight decay of 0.001, and a limit of 500
epochs. Each real trial was retained and supplemented
with 20 noisy copies during training. Independent
zero-mean Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of
\(0.5^\circ\) was added to each angle. A second
\(0.5^\circ\) Gaussian perturbation was shared by the
four joints of each finger within each grip. These artificial copies
approximate small measurement and grasp variation; they
are not counted as additional physical trials. Samples
were weighted inversely to the number of real trials in
their class to reduce the effect of unequal class sizes.

\subsection{Validation and recognition decisions}
\label{sec:validation}

The implemented evaluation uses up to three folds, with
real trials from every class assigned to different folds
before noise is added. Noise augmentation is performed
only on each training fold. The validation fold therefore
contains measurements from the physical hand, not
perturbed copies of training samples. The best epoch in
each fold is selected by validation cross-entropy, with
training stopped after 80 epochs without improvement.
The final model is then trained on all real trials for
the median number of selected epochs.

The class probabilities are adjusted with a temperature
selected from the held-out folds. We also compute the
distance from a new normalized angle vector to the
nearest saved physical training grasp. A prediction is
reported as \emph{unknown} when this distance exceeds
1.5 times the 95th percentile of the validation
distances. Otherwise, it is reported as \emph{uncertain}
when the largest class probability is below 0.75 or
the difference between the two largest probabilities
is below 0.20. A known class is returned only when
these checks pass. These thresholds were chosen using
known-class trials; their ability to reject genuinely
unseen objects has not yet been evaluated.

If a neural prediction remains uncertain, the program
can request another trial of the same object, up to
three trials. Each trial runs the configured grip sequence.
The class probabilities are averaged across trials, while
the nearest-reference distances are combined using their
median. The resulting decision is saved with each trial.
A separate nearest-trial method assigns the class of the saved
physical grasp with the smallest Euclidean distance
in degrees. That method supplies a comparison baseline;
it does not produce probabilities or an unknown-object
decision.

For the completed 20-object study, the primary outcome
will be raw class accuracy on held-out physical trials,
reported with a confusion matrix and per-class results.
We will also report the fraction of predictions accepted
by the decision rule, the accuracy among accepted
predictions, and the effect of repeated grasps. Trials
from later sessions and separate objects excluded from
training are needed to evaluate robustness and
unknown-object rejection independently. The current
folded evaluation does not substitute for those tests.

\subsection{Software implementation}
\label{sec:software}

The grip and physical-only training programs are part
of Ex-ARM \citep{ExARM}. The terminal interface can
collect, resume, extend, and retake physical trials,
train either model, and run the hand for recognition.
The presentation window shows the final decision,
the neural probabilities for every enrolled class,
and a visualization driven by the measured hand pose.
The classifier itself uses only recorded physical angles.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Four-object household pilot}
\label{sec:pilot_results}

The available household dataset contains 12 physical trials:
three each for a ball, bottle, bowl, and computer mouse.
All four labels refer to the specific items recorded.
These measurements used the earlier single-grip, 16-angle
configuration. The current three-grip version of the
software has not yet been evaluated on these objects.

In three-fold validation, the neural classifier assigned
the correct class to 11 of the 12 held-out trials
(91.7\%). The one error assigned a ball trial to the
bottle class. Table~\ref{tab:pilot} gives the
per-class counts. The final model was then trained on
all 12 trials; this training step is distinct from the
held-out evaluation. A nearest-trial baseline also
classified 11 of 12 trials correctly in
leave-one-trial-out evaluation.

When the pilot's probability, margin, and distance rules
were applied, ten of the 12 held-out predictions were
accepted as known classes. All ten accepted predictions
were correct; two were marked uncertain and none were
marked unknown. This 10/10 accepted result is descriptive:
the temperature and distance limit were selected using
the same folded validation predictions. It is not an
independent estimate of the acceptance rule's accuracy.
No item excluded from the four training classes was used
to evaluate unknown-object rejection.

\subsection{Interpretation and study limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The pilot supports a narrow conclusion: final joint angles
from a controlled physical grasp distinguished these four
recorded items in 11 of 12 held-out trials. It does not
show that the classifier can recognize 20 different
household objects. Three trials per class give a limited
view of within-item variation, and the validation trials
came from the same physical exemplars and collection
setting as the training trials. The results do not measure
generalization to another bottle, ball, bowl, or mouse,
to a later session, or to changed placement.

The three-grip design may provide additional information
when one grip leaves two objects in similar joint postures.
It also makes a trial longer and may disturb the object
while the hand returns to the next starting posture.
Those effects must be measured rather than assumed.
The four-object pilot results cannot be transferred to the
three-grip configuration because its inputs, grip motions,
and saved model are different.

For the 20-object study, each class should have several
independent placements, and trials should be collected
across multiple sessions. In addition to a trial-level
split, a later session should be held out until the model
and its decision thresholds are fixed. Reporting the
confusion matrix, per-class recall, overall raw accuracy,
coverage, and accuracy among accepted predictions will
show which item pairs are difficult to separate. Objects
excluded from all training are needed to evaluate the
\emph{unknown} output. Repeated-sequence recognition
should be compared with a single sequence on the same
held-out cases to determine whether another observation
improves the decision.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work develops a physical-only proprioceptive
recognition procedure for a LEAP Hand. Motor-current
thresholds stop contacted fingers, and a compact neural
network predicts object identity from final joint angles.
The software now supports an ordered sequence of three
grips, giving 48 angle inputs per trial, as well as
resumable collection, trial retakes, and repeated
recognition attempts.

The available household pilot used the earlier
single-grip configuration. Its neural classifier
correctly identified 11 of 12 held-out physical trials
from a ball, bottle, bowl, and mouse. Ten of 12 predictions
passed its acceptance rules, and all ten were correct.
These findings establish a promising four-item pilot.
Collecting and evaluating the 20-class, three-grip
dataset is the next step before claiming accurate
recognition across 20 household objects.
